## summary
### LITE
A four-volume set of Korolevskiy Troupe ballet scripts from Snezhnaya (7.0). Vol. 1 is Black Snow Swan, about a black swan who refuses to give up love to become pure; Vols. 2 to 4 hold The Seabird, The Envoy and The Firebird.
### STANDARD
Each volume is an excerpted script with cast, act headings, stage directions and spoken lines, in the manner of a printed libretto. The four ballets are separate stories. Black Snow Swan is the one the game connects to other content, including the character Odette and the sword Whitelake Frostfeather.

## appearance
### STANDARD
A 4-star Book Collection of 4 volumes. The text uses stage directions in parentheses, tempo markings (adagio, presto), named variations and curtain calls, and some acts are skipped with ellipses.

## lore
### LITE
Vol. 1, Black Snow Swan: the dark-feathered swan Odile is told by Queen Myrtha that love is a poison. Three spirits show her love's cost, but she chooses love and flight. Vol. 2, The Seabird: the composer Kostya loses Zarechnaya and himself. Vol. 3, The Envoy: a town panics at a rumoured imperial inspector and is fooled by a thief. Vol. 4, The Firebird: a prince, the Firebird and a captive princess defeat a demon king.
### STANDARD
**Black Snow Swan.** Odile, a black swan among white sisters, believes her colour is a shame. Queen Myrtha says it comes from human emotion, love and passion, a poison that fate uses as a snare, and promises three spirits across three nights. Bathilde de Petrichor (Spring), a Fontainian noble betrayed in love, Hamsatti (Summer), a princess of Pragjyotishpura who destroyed the man she loved and the dancer he loved, and Tatiana (Autumn), a snow swan, each warn that love is sorrow, cruelty and weakness. In Act III Odile answers that love and passion belong to the heart, not to Fate, dances thirty-two fouettes, and refuses eternal purity. Myrtha lets her go. At dawn the black swan sets out to fly higher than any snow swan.
### LARGE +
**The Seabird.** Act IV, in a country estate. Kostya, a composer chafing at popular taste, meets Zarechnaya, once his muse, who left with Boris for Snezhnograd and lost her voice and confidence there. She has learned to sing for the joy of singing and leaves alone to work in Svetloledovka. Kostya says he has killed a seabird; a gunshot is heard and he is seen lying on the stage.

**The Envoy.** In the reign of Emperor Monomakh, Viscount Schpekin's town panics when a dispatch from the Zapolyarny Palace announces an incognito envoy of the Pale Star. A visitor, Khlestakov, is taken for the envoy and leaves with gems, revealing himself as the thief Reed Miller. The Druzhna then arrive for the real envoy, and Schpekin turns on the audience: they are laughing at themselves.

**The Firebird.** Grand Duke Vernov's golden apples are being stolen. His third son Khramov keeps watch and sees the Firebird, Sirin. Vaselisa, a farm girl carried off by the Demon King Berstuk to his diamond castle, refuses his gifts and throws his crown at him; Khramov, with the Mirrorblade and the Firebird, defeats Berstuk. Sirin's closing lines say freedom and love are the dearest things.

**Black Snow Swan details.** Myrtha says mercy and kindness die at the slightest breath and only cold, impartial calculation lasts until dawn, and that love makes the mightiest hero kneel before Fate, a "cruel usurper". Bathilde recounts placing her golden necklace on a village maiden who danced with the hunter she herself was betrothed to. Hamsatti blames "the Lord of vaana" for the arrow that made her love, and warns that even gods once touched by love cannot escape Fate's bargain. Tatiana says the heavens have arranged all and warns that once the oath is broken the skies shall shut. Odile's reply is that love is "the blade that cuts Fate's snare" and that she wants one brief true-blue moment over an empty eternity. Myrtha says she has no right to judge her choice, and Odile says the same of Myrtha.

**The Seabird details.** Zarechnaya quotes lines she once sang on Kostya's bare stage about the earth bearing no living soul for ten million years. The seabird stands for both the bird Kostya killed and Zarechnaya. Boris has returned to Kostya's mother Polina. The line that a man chanced upon a seabird and destroyed it for want of anything else to do recurs at the end.

**The Envoy details.** The officials fall into a panic dance and each fears being slandered by an informer. Khlestakov ends by greeting the room in the name of the ravens, and the aristocrats' elegance dissolves into flight when the Druzhna enter with a decree.

**The Firebird details.** Act II is under the golden apple tree, where Khramov bathes his eyes with dew to stay awake. The cast list names Sirin as the Firebird, Berstuk as Demon King, Vaselisa as Princess, plus Forest Creatures and the Demon King's Minion.
### ULTRA +
**Links the wiki points out.** Whitelake Frostfeather's item text depicts the in-universe inspiration for Tatiana's scene. Odette's Elemental Skill "Adagio: Phantom Night Dancers" quotes Bathilde's closing lines about the candle flame of winter, and Odette's constellation names echo Odile's final speech, per the wiki. Odette shares her name with the heroine of the real-world Swan Lake, in which Odile is a character.

**Interpretive notes, treated as inferred.** The wiki compares Myrtha's poison of love with the Primordial Human's Eitr, which the Belyi Tsar calls love in An Ark That Used to Be a Soul, and the swan's blackening with the Shadow Realm spreading in the Court of Eternal Bliss. It also suggests "the Lord of vaana" refers to the Heavenly Principles. These are readings of the text, not statements in it.

**Real-world references the wiki lists.** Black Snow Swan draws on Swan Lake (Odile, thirty-two fouettes), Giselle (Myrtha, Bathilde) and La Bayadere (Hamsatti), and The Firebird resembles Stravinsky's ballet. They are out-of-universe references. Bathilde's dance is set to a minuet in the style of the Palais Mermonia.

**Use in scene.** The scripts suit a character reading, quoting or explaining a Troupe ballet, but the lines above are one printed version, and the books do not say how any staging differs.
