## summary
### LITE
A two-volume Mondstadt book of ballads handed down from the aristocratic period and said to come from Ragnvindr, the Dawn Knight. Volume 1 follows his time as a knight's squire; Volume 2 his friendship with a sword-dancer.
### STANDARD
Both volumes are found in Dawn Winery, and Volume 1 can also be got from Ellin in the City of Mondstadt. The ballads are sympathetic to the poor and hostile to the old aristocracy.

## appearance
### STANDARD
A 3-star book collection. The text is free-verse poetry in a first-person voice, in short stanzas with heavy imagery of wind, night streets and chains. The cover title in the Teyvat script reads "Breaking Dawn" (wiki trivia).

## lore
### LITE
Vol. 1: a squire patrols Mondstadt at night, ignoring the knights' boasts of starlit glory and seeing only the suffering of the poor under the aristocracy. Vol. 2: a chained dancer of the Wanderer's Troupe arrives, challenges the aristocrats, and dies in the arena; a nameless knight buries her sword.
### STANDARD
**Volume 1.** The narrator walks every street of Mondstadt and finds both the arrogant rich and the pitiful poor. His peers and superiors proclaim that the starlit knights are the city's true protectors, but he never looks up at the stars or the flag, because he cannot look away from the grimy corners where peddlers weep, old soldiers grimace and a girl rejected by the aristocracy prays to Barbatos for mercy. The wind carries the people's grief against the Cathedral gate and the palace walls, but to those inside it is only the sighing of ants.

**Volume 2.** At dawn a sword-dancer, bound in chains, enters Mondstadt, her silence already a song of freedom. She belongs to the Wanderer's Troupe and is a "dawnlight" to the people but eternal midnight to the aristocracy. The narrator asks why she would overthrow them; she answers by asking why he puts their lives on a pedestal, and whether a man who calls the wind his companion has never known freedom himself. She tells her listeners of the rulers' better ancestors who held divine power, and of angels, gods, dragons and the peoples of the land, turning each legend into song for the wind to carry. In the aristocrats' arena she sings with her sword one last time, her final work stopping short of perfection, and a nameless knight recovers her sword from the blood-soaked battlefield and buries it where the gentle winds meet.
### LARGE +
**Reading the poems.** The ballads work as a counter-narrative to the aristocratic period's self-image. The "starlit knights" boast in Volume 1 is quoted only to be set aside by the narrator. The dancer's argument in Volume 2 turns on the wind, the symbol of Mondstadt's freedom, to question the aristocrats' claim to rule, and her stories of divine ancestors show the troupe preserving older myth in song. The ending is understated: the narrator does not say he is the knight who buries the sword.

**Attribution.** The in-game descriptions make authorship a tradition, not a fact. Ragnvindr appears as both poet and squire. The sword-dancer is linked on the wiki to the Dawnlight Swordswoman, and her sword to The Flute.
### ULTRA +
**Limits for portrayal.** No date, no master of the squire and no name for the dancer are given. The Church of Favonius is not named in the text; only the Cathedral gate appears, so the poems cannot be used to say how the Church behaved in that period. Avoid giving the narrator extra biography beyond what the Ragnvindr entry supports.

## owners
### STANDARD
No character is established as owning it. The Traveler can pick the volumes up in Dawn Winery, and Volume 1 can also come from Ellin in the City of Mondstadt.

## gameplay
### STANDARD
Game mechanics: a two-volume Book Collection, rarity 3, with no combat function. Both volumes lie inside Dawn Winery; Volume 1 has a second route through a dialogue path with Ellin. Added in version 1.0.

### STANDARD
The ballads are said to come from Ragnvindr, the Dawn Knight, and tell of him as a knight's squire in the aristocratic era. The first volume shows him sympathising with the poor and refusing to join his superiors' boasting; the second shows him befriending a Wanderer's Troupe sword-dancer. This is a tradition, not confirmed authorship. His other deeds are covered on his own entry.
