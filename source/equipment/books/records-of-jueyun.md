## summary
### LITE
Records of Jueyun is a six-volume book collection of folk stories, myths and legends of Liyue by an author named Wuyuan. Each volume handles one tale: living stone beasts, a Sea God who takes brides, the haunted ruins of Wuwang Hill, the lonely seelie of the mountains, the gentle qilin, and the ancient Dunyu Ruins. The in-game blurb calls it quite a page turner.
### STANDARD +
The collection shows how ordinary Liyue people explain their landscape, and what local belief says about adepti and illuminated beasts. It is hearsay, not history: the compilers repeatedly hedge.

## appearance
### STANDARD
Six short prose pieces in a storyteller's voice, each under a single-title heading (Stone Beasts, Palace Beneath the Sea, Wuwang, Mountain Spirits, Qilin, Hidden Jade). The narrator mixes local rumour with dry asides, such as noting that nobody has actually seen a stone beast move.

## lore
@spoiler: minor
### LITE
- Vol. 1, Stone Beasts: Liyue's animal statues are said to wake on cool autumn nights and wander. Qingce elders say they are illuminated beasts who fought beside Rex Lapis in the Archon War and asked him to turn their mortal flesh to lasting stone.
- Vol. 2, Palace Beneath the Sea: a Sea God collects a village bride each festival in return for calm seas. One bride is released, keeps his sea-snail shell, and years later blows it when her own daughter is to be given up; the village is drowned.
- Vol. 3, Wuwang: Wuwang Hill, north of Mt. Qingce, is haunted by spirits that lure travellers off the path.
- Vol. 4, Mountain Spirits: seelie are held to be good omens that guide travellers and Vision-bearers to treasure.
- Vol. 5, Qilin: gentle illuminated beasts of the mountains who sometimes take human form and live among mortals.
- Vol. 6, Hidden Jade: the Dunyu Ruins, north of Nantianmen and northwest of Liyue Harbor, were a walled city whose people sealed it and left.
### STANDARD +
- Vol. 1 says locals who know the land have long noted that animal statues change place and posture overnight, and that campers hear a deep sound like flowing water. Bishui River fishermen, Silvergrass harvesters of Dihua Marsh and old miners share the story. The narrator admits nobody has witnessed a stone beast moving.
- Vol. 2 ends cruelly: the Sea God's tidal embrace swallows the sleeping village and its elders, and mother and daughter ride his clam carriage into the sea. The Sea God had earlier said his palace sheltered women who were driven from their villages.
- Vol. 3 explains the name: Wuwang means prudence, so the hill is where the prudent are punished. A village lies abandoned at its foot. A Qingce children's fable says its young people threw themselves into the Bishui River, enchanted by a distant sea monster's song, and drifted to the Sea of Clouds. The text then offers an explanation for the haunting: the ley lines remember what people forget, and unconsciously recreate the dead village's past as spirits.
- Vol. 4 gives two readings of the seelie: auspicious souls of departed adepti or old benevolent gods, or echoes of friendless strangers who guide the lost home. A mountain legend adds that seelie once had beautiful bodies and great wisdom, long before the adepti, and links their ancestor to a traveller from the Sea of Stars, an oath witnessed by the three sisters of the Lunar Palace, and a catastrophe thirty days later that separated them and wiped their memories. The narrator dismisses all this as incoherent folk tale.
- Vol. 5 says that after the Archon War many adepti withdrew from society at Rex Lapis' direction, but some illuminated beasts chose to stay and help mortals, leaving unusual bloodlines in Liyue Harbor. A tale of a young qilin who ate a herb gatherer's lotus-leaf clothes at a mountain pool and took human form has two endings: she leaves a child in a basket at his door, or she stays and raises children with him.
- Vol. 6 says Dunyu means the land to which beauteous jade flees. The legend tells of a star that fell onto the plains west of Liyue and made the chasm, from which jade emerged and supported a thousand years of mining. A fragment fell in northern Lisha and later leapt back to the sky, leaving a pit where humans built fortresses. These kept good relations with Liyue Harbor until about 500 years ago, when the black calamity and the rise of the Abyss led the residents to seal the city and leave. The text says even the old adepti and yaksha are silent about why.
### LARGE +
- Vol. 1 and the book Yakshas: The Guardian Adepti both describe illuminated beasts serving Rex Lapis: in one they become stone, in the other warriors who suffer under the lingering bitterness of defeated gods.
- Vol. 4 is explicitly framed as legend about a time before Rex Lapis, and the narrator stresses it lacks credibility. Use it for atmosphere or in-world rumour, not as confirmed history of the seelie.
### ULTRA +
- The wiki notes that Vol. 4's claim that seelie guide only Vision-bearers is misleading: seelie also guide people without Visions in other canon.
- Vol. 6's reference to the black calamity places the sealing of Dunyu around the Cataclysm, about 500 years ago. Use the Dunyu Ruins and Cataclysm entries for details beyond this book.

## owners

## entry: The Sea God's Bride
@keys: Palace Beneath the Sea, Sea God's bride
@keys2: Records of Jueyun
### STANDARD
A Liyue folk tale in Records of Jueyun Vol. 2. Each festival a village sends a bride to a Sea God in exchange for a year without storms. One bride is freed and given a conch to sound when she regrets leaving; she does so when the elders come for her daughter, and the Sea God drowns the village. The god is not identified.

## entry: Wuwang Hill Legend
@keys: Wuwang Hill, hill where the prudent are punished
@keys2: ghosts, spirits, Qingce
### STANDARD
Per Records of Jueyun Vol. 3, Wuwang Hill is haunted by lingering spirits that imitate the voices of the dead and lead travellers astray. The book gives a Qingce children's fable and an explanation that the ley lines unconsciously replay the abandoned village's past.

## entry: Dunyu Ruins Legend
@keys: Dunyu Ruins, Dunyu Valley
@keys2: Records of Jueyun, fallen star
### STANDARD
Records of Jueyun Vol. 6 says Dunyu is the land to which beauteous jade flees. A star fell on the plains west of Liyue, making the chasm; a fragment fell in northern Lisha, and the Dunyu fortresses were built in the pit it left. Their people sealed the city and left about 500 years ago, at the time of the black calamity; the reason is not known, even to old adepti and yaksha.
