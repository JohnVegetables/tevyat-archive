## summary
### LITE
Hymns of the Far North is a three-volume book of prayer songs of the Frostmoon Scions, compiled by the priestess Ehrnrooth and found at the Frostmoon Enclave (added in 6.0). Most of its rites are long disused. Each old prayer is followed by Ehrnrooth's note on how the first Moonchanter, Aila, replaced it with a short prayer to the pale Frost Moon.
### STANDARD
The collection preserves the Scions' pre-Aila religion, which prayed to the gods of the far north with offerings and divination, and shows how Aila, five centuries ago, abolished or simplified those rites around the Frost Moon. Its tone is reverent and sorrowful: the singers are exiled heirs of Hyperborea who cannot go home while the moon is imprisoned.

## appearance
### STANDARD
A 4-star book collection of three volumes with a verse opening, then numbered Prayer Songs. Each song gives the god's name, a parenthetical description of the rite (date, offerings, how many times the Runo is chanted), the hymn, and a note from Priestess Ehrnrooth. Some lines are blacked out.

## lore
### LITE
A priestess's collection of hymns to the old gods of the far north, each with a note explaining how and why the first Moonchanter simplified it. It reflects the Scions' faith before and after Aila.
### STANDARD
The singer of the opening hymn is an exile who cannot return home while the moon is imprisoned. The numbered Prayer Songs address northern gods at solstices, births and funerals, beginning with Pitkamoonen, Lord of the Seven Calamities, then Maadteraahka, Tuonetar and Laimelea; Vol. 2 and 3 include Ianikuinen, Koitar, Saarelainen, Huurrekuutar and the Moon Maiden Kuutar. Ehrnrooth's notes explain how Aila replaced them with short prayers to the Frost Moon.
### LARGE +
**Opening hymn.** A traveler's lament: the singer has left home and cannot return while "moonlight hangs in its prison on high". It praises rivers, peaks, the bronze-bound ship of Seutervoinen and the golden arrows of Ianikuinen, curses the poisonous plot of Pakkaisukko and the calamity of Pohjola, and calls the heirs of Hyperborea to remember that so long as hymn and Runo ring, the far North will endure.

**Vol. 1.** Prayer Song I appeases Pitkamoonen, Lord of the Seven Calamities, at the summer solstice, with a burnt sheaf, a hen, an ox and blood, the Archpriestess chanting the Runo seven times and reading entrails. Song II honors Maadteraahka, Mother of Life, at births. Song III is to Tuonetar, Mother of the Underworld, at autumn funerals. Song IV is to Laimelea, Mother of Time, on the winter solstice. Song V is missing. Ehrnrooth notes that Aila abolished the rites for the first three five centuries ago, replacing them with short blessings of the new moon.

**Vol. 2.** Song VI is to Takoja Ianikuinen, the Primeval Blacksmith, whose forged bow, chariot and shuttle were each stolen or lost. Song XI is to Koitar, the dawnstar, wife of Seutervoinen. Song XVI is to Saarelainen, the Stranger of Winter's Day, who rode to Pohjola to court the daughter of Pakkaisukko and was carved into seven pieces by the Lord of the Seven Calamities.

**Vol. 3.** Song XXI is to Huurrekuutar, goddess of the Frost Moon; Song XXII is to Kuutar, the Moon Maiden, asking for mercy and for the cleansing of those barred from Elysium.
**Aila's revisions.** For Songs VI, XI, XVI and XXI Ehrnrooth prints both the older form and "the standard for all recitations", a short prayer that begins "O pale white Frost Moon, in the name of the descendants of the far north" and asks the moon to soothe wrath, protect the descendants, heal the lost, or cast down its gaze from the celestial dome. For Song VI Ehrnrooth adds that a lost variant, whose last four lines called disasters rather than quelled them, vanished long before the Moon Maiden's birth. For Song XXI she says the old prayer was brief because only the protecting chief god and the moons' origin, past and future were too sacred to say at length.

**Rites in detail.** Song I includes a note that a god of unknowable gender is addressed and no one dares speak the name. Song II sprinkles sea salt on the sacred stone and bathes the newborn in water with Winter Icelea. Song III has animals sacrificed in boat-shaped vessels according to the dead's rank, with their eyes presented to the underworld's mistress and the Archpriestess veiled. Song IV burns a cloth of the year's events and anoints the Verdant Crest with the ashes in silver water.

**The Song of Saarelainen.** The hymn identifies Pakkaisukko (wiki: the Belyi Tsar) as the servant of the singers' forebears who offered his daughter in marriage if Saarelainen would open the gates of Pohjola, knowing he would be sacrificed. The Lord of the Seven Calamities then cut him into seven pieces and his bride was never wed. The wiki links the daughter to Anastasya and the gates to the Shadow Realm; the hymn itself names neither.
### ULTRA +
**Reading it as history.** The collection layers three periods: the exiles' old religion in Hyperborea, the long wait before the Moon Maiden, and Aila's reform after Kuutar's birth. The notes consistently credit Aila with turning a fearful, sacrificial religion into one directed to the Frost Moon alone, and the Scions' present prayers are those "standard" forms. Ehrnrooth also flags what she cannot explain (Song IV's reference to the four imprisoned moons and the Verdant Crest) and keeps it as written. Prayer Song XXII's simplified form matches a prayer the wiki says Lauma recites in the Song of the Welkin Moon teaser; Pitkamoonen's Chinese transliteration is a pun on Paimon's name per the wiki trivia, a translator's joke rather than lore.

**Link to the Heavenly Principles.** The wiki identifies the supreme sky god Pitkamoonen, "merciless" and "capricious winged", as the Heavenly Principles; Song IV's Mother of Time as Istaroth; Maadteraahka as Naberius (with a "heart for the primordial ocean" line linked to Egeria); and Tuonetar as Ronova. These are wiki identifications; the hymns themselves only supply the northern names.

## entry: Aila's reform of the Frostmoon rites
@keys: Aila, first Moonchanter, Moonchanter
@keys2: hymn, prayer, rite, Ehrnrooth
@version: 6.0
@availability: permanent
### STANDARD
In Ehrnrooth's notes, Aila, the first Moonchanter, abolished the old rites to the Seven Calamities, the Mother of Life and the Mother of the Underworld five centuries ago, replacing them with brief blessings such as "May the new moon illuminate your path". She revised and simplified the prayers to Ianikuinen, Koitar, Saarelainen and Huurrekuutar into short forms addressed to the pale white Frost Moon, which are now the standard recitation.
### LARGE +
For funerals the replacement is "May the new moon lead you to the shore of peace," followed by a simple and solemn funeral regardless of social status.

### STANDARD
The first prayer song, performed at the summer solstice, begs a merciless sky god to turn from the mortal realm. Its offerings include grain, a hen and an ox. Portions of its text are blacked out because the Archpriestess chanted indistinctly so as not to provoke the god. The wiki identifies the god as the Heavenly Principles.

### STANDARD
Prayer Song XVI honors Saarelainen, a gentle noble who rode across Hiisi Island and Pohjola to court Pakkaisukko's daughter. Pakkaisukko demanded he open the gates of Pohjola. Saarelainen went knowingly, to spare the girl, and the Lord of the Seven Calamities cut him into seven pieces. The wiki links Pakkaisukko to the Belyi Tsar and his daughter to Anastasya; the hymn does not name them.

## entry: Frostmoon Scions' prayer to the Frost Moon

### STANDARD
Prayer Song XXI honors Huurrekuutar, goddess of the Frost Moon, "mother of the silver shuttle" who leads lost souls through the cold night, and Song XXII addresses Kuutar, the Moon Maiden, asking her to cleanse sins and the tears of those barred from Elysium. The standard form beseeches the pale white Frost Moon to cast down its gaze from the celestial dome.
