## summary
### LITE
A playwright of Fontaine who lived about 500 years ago and wrote prolifically from childhood. His last opera, "Clockwork Coppelia", was premiering when the opera house was destroyed, and he is presumed to have died there. His name lives on through the weapon Flowing Purity and the Icewind Suite challenge.
### STANDARD +
He is known only through texts that others wrote: item descriptions, the Institute of Natural Philosophy's Ancient Notes, and the reconstructed script of his last work.

## background
### LITE
A talented writer from childhood, linked to the Institute of Natural Philosophy, who adapted legends and historical figures into plays. Late in life his works circled themes of eternal beauty and eternal love, which contemporary critics noticed.
### STANDARD +
His known works include "Golden Hyperborea", "Coppelia" and "Masque of the Great". "Golden Hyperborea" imagines a homesick Lumidouce Bell, called the "Weeping Crystal", whose beauty is made permanent by icy winds in Hyperborea, an old name wiki sources connect to Snezhnaya. Another play was loosely drawn from the fallen noble Parsifal and the Blue-Eyed Spear Witch of the Mondstadt aristocracy era, and inspired the stage names Parsifal the Great and Josephine.
### LARGE +
"Clockwork Coppelia" was his final opera. It was premiered before the cataclysm 500 years ago. After the intermission something went wrong and the opera house was destroyed, along with many performers and spectators. Its script was never published; a company later gathered survivors' memories of the pre-intermission scenes and reconstructed part of it with the consent of Coppelius's family.
### ULTRA +
The reconstructed excerpts feature Coppelius himself as a character: his scheme is exposed to the Marechaussee Hunters and he is arrested and parted from his creation, the automaton Coppelia, whom he calls his love, sin and soul. In a second scene Coppelia tells a young man, Nathaniel, that she means to follow her creator, who she believes has died. The recreators warn that this is only an attempt to recreate his work.

## relationships
### STANDARD
Dwight Lasker, director of the Institute of Natural Philosophy, writes in the Ancient Notes that he was looking forward to Coppelius's new work and that Coppelius has always been wonderfully talented. The Institute of Natural Philosophy is listed as his former affiliation.
### LARGE +
Bossuet, a researcher at the Fontaine Research Institute, drew on "Clockwork Coppelia" to build the Icewind Suite, a staged challenge of mechanical dancers. After Bossuet left, Maillardet took it over and gave the dancers combat abilities.

## status
### LITE
Presumed dead, killed when the opera house was destroyed on the day his last opera premiered about 500 years ago.
### STANDARD +
No source describes his death directly. His work survives in a reconstructed script, in the Flowing Purity weapon, and in the Icewind Suite.

## mistakes
### STANDARD
- Do not give him a voice, personality profile or romance: he is only known through texts.
- The excerpts in Flowing Purity are a reconstruction; do not present them as his authentic script.
- Do not assert that the opera house was destroyed by the cataclysm or by any named cause.
- Do not confuse him with the automaton Coppelia or with the Icewind Suite boss, which is a later creation.

## chronology
### LARGE
- Childhood: shows a talent for playwriting; Dwight Lasker later remarks on it.
- Later life: writes "Golden Hyperborea" and other works on eternal beauty and love.
- Shortly before the cataclysm 500 years ago: premieres "Clockwork Coppelia"; the opera house is destroyed.
- Later: a company reconstructs part of the script; Bossuet and then Maillardet build the Icewind Suite.
- 3.6: the Icewind Suite is introduced.
- 4.0: Dwight's Ancient Notes and the Flowing Purity weapon text appear.

## entry: Golden Hyperborea
@keys: Golden Hyperborea, Weeping Crystal
@version: 4.0
### STANDARD
A play by Coppelius about a homesick Lumidouce Bell, called the Weeping Crystal, wandering the freezing north. The icy winds preserve its beauty forever. Critics of his day said the play showed his obsession with eternal beauty and eternal love.
