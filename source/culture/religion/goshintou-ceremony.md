## summary
### LITE
The Goshintou Ceremony is a solemn Inazuman rite honouring the arrival of the Shogun, who according to tradition brought the knowledge of sword-making to the land. Master swordsmiths present their finest blades, called Goshintou, which are offered to the Grand Narukami Shrine. The swords stand for the union of human craft and the Shogun's divine favour.
### STANDARD +
Canon says very little about it directly: the best source is a passage in the Inazuman novel Treasured Tales of the Chouken Shinkageuchi, and no quest shows the ceremony being held.

## practice
### STANDARD
Per the book, the ceremony began after thousands of years and many harvests, when mortal smiths could at last forge swords pleasing even to Narukami. The Grand Narukami Shrine and the Shogunate then gathered the greatest swords in the world and offered them to the shrine, and the blades so offered were named Goshintou. The book calls the occasion lively and says the custom continues in the present.
### LARGE +
The book adds a hidden side to the tradition. A famous craftsman never makes a single blade in one sitting. The very best work is a Shinuchi, given a fine name and presented to the craftsman's lord or deity; it is not made for taking lives and stays pure. Other blades from the same smith are Kageuchi, given to retainers as working weapons that will shed blood and be stained. The author says few people know these behind-the-scenes matters. The book does not say outright that Goshintou are always Shinuchi, though that is the natural reading of the passage.

## regional_variation
### LARGE
No regional variation is described. The offering goes to the Grand Narukami Shrine on Narukami Island, the seat of the Electro Archon's worship, so the rite belongs to the Shogunate's heartland rather than to Watatsumi, Tsurumi or other islands with their own faiths.

## scene_use
### STANDARD
Use it as background colour for Inazuman sword culture: a smith talking about preparing a piece worthy of the shrine, a collector explaining the difference between Shinuchi and Kageuchi, or a priest of the shrine receiving a blade. Keep dates, rankings and rules vague unless the scene invents them clearly as local detail, since canon gives none.
### LARGE +
Characters who know the book can repeat its version of the origin story, but should treat it as popular legend: it is a novel that was once restricted, and Ei herself calls its later parts idealised. A character close to the Shrine or Shogunate would present the ceremony simply as a respectful custom, with no claim about what the Shogun thinks of it.

## examples
### ULTRA
- A smith's apprentice asks why their master treats the best blade of a forging differently from the rest of the batch (the Shinuchi and Kageuchi distinction).
- A traveller in Inazuma City hears a bookseller describe the Goshintou as swords "too clean for any battlefield" and is told the story comes from a novel, not a chronicle.

## entry: Shinuchi and Kageuchi
@keys: Shinuchi, Kageuchi, Goshintou, Shinkageuchi
@version: 2.2
### STANDARD
In the account given by Treasured Tales of the Chouken Shinkageuchi, a master smith's best sword is a Shinuchi, named and offered to a lord or deity and kept free of bloodshed, while the other swords from the same forging are Kageuchi, issued to retainers and used in battle. Goshintou are the swords offered to the Grand Narukami Shrine; the book frames the offering as the origin of the name.
### LARGE +
The same passage ties the terms to the first Shogun and her younger twin, Shin and Kage, light and dark; this is spoiler-adjacent context for the novel's title. See the book's own entry for that story.
